## The visual system

This is not a plain whiteboard animation. It alternates three authored worlds.

### 1. Recurring classroom host set

A caveman-like teacher occupies a warm ochre classroom with a large green
chalkboard, wooden frame, desks, pointer, small projector/radio, and recurring wall
sign. Historical images or paintings appear *inside the board* while the host
continues speaking. The classroom is a stable visual home base and chapter reset.

The host has a relatively detailed, semi-realistic head—hair mass, beard texture,
wrinkles, eye whites/irises, long nose, asymmetrical mouth—on an intentionally crude
body: cropped torso, leaf belt, and single black noodle arms. Glasses and pointer
mark the teaching role. This high-detail-head/low-detail-body mismatch is central
to the comedy.

### 2. Immersive story-world plates

Historical scenes use full 16:9 painted plates: cottages, stone rooms, laboratories,
villages, rivers, ice fields, caves, mountains and maps. Environments have more
construction and depth than characters, but remain flat 2D illustrations with dark
contours, broad color regions and restrained shading. The plate usually remains
stable while character/prop cutouts enter, translate, scale, rotate, swap poses, or
multiply.

### 3. White explanation cards

Roughly 29% mean white area across shot samples comes from frequent white cards.
They carry one isolated noun, number, diagram, map, timeline, object, comparison, or
literal punchline. Text is sparse, black, irregular and hand-lettered. Cards are
not subtitles. They reset visual complexity and make the next immersive plate feel
richer.

## Character construction

### Proportion and silhouette

- Heads occupy roughly 45–75% of total character height.
- Head silhouettes are specific and asymmetric: irregular hair clumps, beard edge,
  brow ridge, nose profile, ear and skull shape.
- Bodies are rectangular/trapezoidal costume blocks with little anatomy.
- Arms are thin near-black curves; hands may be tiny, oversized, or replaced by a
  literal photographic/illustrated hand for a gag.
- Legs are often absent, hidden by a robe/torso, or represented by two short strokes.
- Characters are readable at very small map/timeline scale and at extreme close-up.

### Face system

The face does most of the acting. Build each recurring character with source-swap
sets rather than free deformation:

- eyes: neutral, glance-left, glance-right, narrowed, wide, closed;
- brows: neutral, raised, compressed/angry, worried asymmetric;
- mouths: closed, small-open, medium-open, wide-open, grimace/smile;
- head: front/three-quarter plus one or two tilt extremes;
- overlays: tears, stink/germ marks, dirt, wounds, glasses, plague mask.

Reference close-ups show the skull, beard and hair staying locked while only mouth,
eyes, eyelids, brows, or a semantic overlay changes. Lip sync is grouped, not
phoneme-perfect. Several frames can hold the same face before a discrete swap.

### Body acting

The acting language is deliberately economical:

- whole-character x/y translation for walking or approaching;
- scale change for depth, emphasis or a crude push toward camera;
- head/torso rotation for lean, collapse and reaction;
- one arm rotation/translation for pointing, holding a mug, using a tool;
- pose replacement for fall, lie-down, death, recoil or costume change;
- clones appearing sequentially for crowds, population and spread;
- body held while eyes/mouth deliver the thought;
- secondary motion limited to fire, smoke, rain, germs, loose arms or a prop.

A complex action is communicated through 2–4 golden states and long holds, not
continuous full-body interpolation. In the drought sequence, a standing character
translates across the fixed plate, swaps to a horizontal collapsed drawing, holds,
then receives a camera punch-in. In the operation sequence, the doctor's head/arm
rotate and eye sources swap while the patient remains a mostly fixed cutout with
small head/expression changes.

## Motion and camera

Across 31,334 adjacent-frame pairs:

- 56.50% are near-frozen;
- 32.64% contain subtle change;
- 10.86% contain active change.

This does **not** mean shots are lifeless. Motion is concentrated into short semantic
events surrounded by readable holds.

Camera behavior:

- locked plate is the base condition;
- slow push-ins of roughly 1–8% occur on host faces, props and environmental beats;
- occasional stronger punch-in follows a reveal, collapse or alarming fact;
- slow lateral reframe follows a timeline, character approach or scene expansion;
- pull-back reveals more huts, people, distance or scale;
- camera and actor rarely peak simultaneously unless the collision is the joke;
- no cinematic orbit, depth-of-field, motion blur, handheld drift or constant
  parallax stack.

A flattened transform detector labeled 55–67% of shots as possible camera moves,
but foreground translation creates false positives. Treat that range only as an
upper bound. Motion strips confirm many scenes are locked plates with independently
moving cutouts, while subtle genuine pushes are common enough to be part of the
language.

## Editing and timing

Detected boundary changed-area classes across 229 events:

| Boundary class | Count | Share |
|---|---:|---:|
| full-frame hard change | 165 | 72.05% |
| broad same-world hard change | 46 | 20.09% |
| local source swap/pop | 15 | 6.55% |
| subtle detected boundary | 3 | 1.31% |

Production implications:

- default to a hard cut;
- retain a location for several narration clauses by changing local states inside
  the shot;
- use 3–6 s as the normal shot envelope;
- use 0.13–1.0 s beats only for a word card, facial sting, static/glitch gag, or
  emphatic correction;
- allow 8–15 s compositions when several assets enter in sequence;
- an exceptional timeline/relationship composition may last 18–25 s while its
  internal states keep changing;
- do not dissolve between ordinary ideas.

## Storytelling grammar

The references repeatedly use this loop:

1. **Timeline or absurd hook.** Place a modern viewer surrogate beside historical
   ancestors, then exaggerate the contrast.
2. **Host thesis.** Return to the classroom and state the chapter/question.
3. **Numbered chapter or named concept.** White card or chalkboard title.
4. **Immersive setup.** Establish a location with a wide world plate.
5. **Character problem.** One or two recurring figures act out the mechanism.
6. **Diagram/comparison.** Cut to white for a noun, number, timeline, map or scale.
7. **Escalation.** Add props, clones, hazards, weather or bodily consequences.
8. **Literal visual joke.** Treat a phrase literally, use an anachronism, or hold
   an uncomfortable expression after the narration moves on.
9. **Teacher correction.** Return to the host/board for the actual explanation.
10. **Callback/payoff.** Reuse an earlier character, prop or location with changed
    meaning, then close in the classroom.

The second reference is list-driven (numbered deadly periods); the first is a
single-topic causal narrative. Both use the same classroom/white-card/story-world
visual alternation.

## Art language

Measured at 640×360 source:

- near-black/dark-brown contour median: 2–2.8 px;
- practical 1080p equivalent: approximately 6–8 px;
- single clean irregular contour; no repeated pencil scribble;
- significant colors per sampled shot: median 14–17;
- earthy dominant family: `#101010`, `#303010`, `#503010`, `#505030`,
  `#705030`, `#707050`, `#907030`, `#909070`, `#D09050`, `#F0D0B0`;
- white cards: `#F0F0F0`/paper white;
- water/ice/map accents: muted cyan/blue; hazard accents: restrained red/orange;
- world plates may use gentle value gradients and atmospheric bands;
- character fills remain mostly flat with limited cheek/hair/clothing texture.

Avoid glossy vector gradients, perfect geometric curves, plastic 3D shading,
photorealistic characters, generic corporate icons and AI-smoothed skin.

## Text and evidence imagery

Use three text voices:

1. irregular black handwritten print on white cards;
2. white chalk script on green board;
3. compact factual labels/arrows next to isolated objects.

Text is image content, not captions. Keep it to a noun, short phrase, number or
question. Historical paintings/photos may appear only as framed evidence on the
chalkboard or as clearly differentiated reference inserts; do not make the whole
video a slideshow.

## Reconstruction stack

1. Write narration and mark host/story/white-card mode for every clause.
2. Build a recurring classroom plate and host head/body rig first.
3. Make original character bibles with locked skull/hair/beard identity.
4. Generate or draw full environment plates separately from characters.
5. Prepare RGBA expression heads, mouths, eyes, arms, props and pose swaps.
6. Re-ink generated construction art with `expressive-doodle-acting` where needed.
7. Animate with `ae-motion`: source swaps, whole-layer transforms, 1–3 local parts.
8. Use HyperFrames only for deterministic timeline/map/layout or motivated global
   camera movement.
9. Hard-cut between modes and retain long internal-state compositions.
10. Validate identity, text, line weight, holds, audio pauses and callback reuse.

Reproduce the measured technique with original characters, scripts, classrooms,
world plates, jokes and branding. Do not trace or clone exact reference drawings.
