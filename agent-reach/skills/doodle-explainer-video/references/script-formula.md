# Script formula

Reverse-engineered from the reference's full narration (~2,200 words, 609 s at
~217 wpm). The structure is the product here — the visuals are subordinate to it.

## Budget

| Target length | Words | Sections | Illustrations |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60 s | ~215 | 3–4 | ~15 |
| 3 min | ~650 | 8–10 | ~45 |
| 10 min | ~2,200 | 18–25 | ~150 |

Divide by ~217 wpm, and allow one illustration per 12–15 words.

## The spine

The reference follows a nine-move arc. Each move has a job; keep the jobs even
if you change the subject.

**1. Sensory cold open (0–15 s).** Second person, present tense, no preamble.
Put the viewer bodily inside a scene before naming the topic.

> "Imagine you are floating in the open ocean. The water is dark and cold
> beneath you. Something massive moves below."

**2. Escalating specificity (15–35 s).** Stack hard numbers, each bigger than
the last. Numbers read as authority.

> "Forty feet long, twelve thousand pounds. It can detect your outline from a
> hundred meters away using nothing but sound."

**3. The impossible fact (35–60 s).** State the paradox that the whole video
exists to resolve, then flatly assert it should not be true. Short fragments.

> "In all of recorded human history, no wild orca has ever killed a person. Not
> once. That should not be possible."

**4. Kill the obvious answer + promise (60–90 s).** Name the explanation the
viewer is already forming and reject it. Then make an explicit retention
promise that ties the topic to the viewer's own life.

> "Most people assume we just don't taste right, but that's not quite right...
> Stay until the end because the last part will change how you think about the
> invisible rules running your life right now."

**5. Reframe the subject.** Correct a misconception before building on it. This
buys credibility for everything after.

> "When you hear the words killer whale, you probably imagine something mindless
> and brutal... That image is wrong."

**6. Evidence stacking.** The bulk of the runtime. Each piece of evidence gets a
named source, an institution, and a date — then a one-line direct address that
tells the viewer how to feel.

> "Researchers at the University of British Columbia... since the nineteen
> seventies documented pods working together to create waves that knock seals
> off ice floes."
>
> "Let that sit with you for a second."

**7. Mid-video re-hook (~50% mark).** Explicitly declare the story is only half
told and pivot the question. This is where retention dies without intervention.

> "But that's only half the story because the question is not just why they
> don't eat us, it's what they actually think of us."

**8. The natural experiment.** Find the case that isolates the variable, and
state the contrast in fragments.

> "Wild orcas never once. Captive orcas repeatedly. What does that tell you?"

**9. The turn to the viewer (final 15%).** Announce that the subject was never
the point, generalise the mechanism to the viewer's own life, and close on an
unanswerable question. The question is comment bait — do not answer it.

> "And this is the moment where this story stops being about an animal."
>
> "What are the rules running you, and who made them?"

## Sentence craft

- **Second person throughout.** "You" appears constantly. Never "one" or "we"
  when "you" will do.
- **Fragments for emphasis.** "Not once." "Yes, moose." A fragment after a long
  sentence lands like a cut.
- **One idea per sentence.** Average sentence is short; the rhythm is
  short-short-long, then a fragment.
- **Concrete over abstract.** "The size of a bathtub", not "a small enclosure".
- **Named sources.** Real researchers, real institutions, real dates. This is
  what separates the format from slop.
- **Rhetorical questions as pivots.** "But why?" "What does that tell you?" Each
  one opens the next section.
- **Numbers spelled out for TTS.** The reference narration says "nineteen
  seventies" and "twelve thousand pounds", not "1970s" or "12,000 lbs". Write
  the script the way it should be read aloud.

## Topic selection

The format needs a subject with a genuine, resolvable paradox, and a mechanism
that generalises to the viewer's life. The orca video works because:

- The central fact is verifiable and surprising (zero wild fatalities).
- The obvious explanation is wrong (taste), so there is somewhere to go.
- The real mechanism (cultural transmission) applies to the viewer.

Test any topic against those three before writing. If the answer is boring or
the mechanism does not generalise, the last 15% collapses and so does retention.

## Accuracy

Every named study, researcher, date, and statistic in the reference is real and
checkable. Keep it that way — verify claims before writing them into the script,
and drop any fact you cannot source. Fabricated citations delivered in this
confident a register are exactly the failure mode to avoid, and a single
debunked claim in the comments undoes the video's credibility.
