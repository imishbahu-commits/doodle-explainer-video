# Research Notes — Bataspis crux

## Primary study

- Meng, Xinyuan; Ferrón, Humberto G.; Pisani, Davide; Gai, Zhikun; Donoghue, Philip C. J. (2026).
- **Title:** *Bataspis crux*, a galeaspid (jawless stem gnathostome) from the Lochkovian of Yunnan with a batwing-shaped head adapted for hydrodynamic efficiency.
- **Journal:** *Palaeontology* 69(4), e70081.
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1111/pala.70081
- **Publication date:** August 4, 2026.

## Verified facts used in the script

- *Bataspis crux* is a new eugaleaspid jawless stem-gnathostome from the Early Devonian Xishancun Formation near Qujing, Yunnan, China.
- The rocks are Lochkovian in age, approximately 419 million years old.
- The fossil was collected in the 1980s and is housed at the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology, Chinese Academy of Sciences.
- The holotype and only known specimen is a nearly complete headshield and counterpart.
- The headshield is 49.6 mm in maximum length, 45.8 mm along the midline, and estimated at approximately 143 mm between the outer tips based on bilateral symmetry.
- The headshield has an unusual batwing/crescent outline formed by cornual and inner cornual processes.
- Galeaspids are extinct jawless fishes endemic to what is now China and northern Vietnam during the Silurian and Devonian.
- The researchers built a three-dimensional full-body reconstruction and tested it with computational fluid dynamics.
- The oralobranchial chamber and missing body components required reconstruction from related galeaspids.
- The model achieved its maximum lift-to-drag ratio above 4.5 at a 10-degree angle of attack.
- This was the highest lift-to-drag ratio among all galeaspids tested; the second-highest was close to 3 at 10 degrees.
- The authors attribute performance to low drag, passive lift from the airfoil-like cross-section, high aspect ratio, crescent planform, and smooth integration of the processes with the headshield.
- Simulations with a virtual ground plane produced nearly the same lift-to-drag pattern, suggesting the modeled efficiency did not depend on one narrowly benthic condition.
- The paper interprets the animal as a potentially effective gliding cruiser and concludes that lacking flexible paired fins did not prevent locomotor/ecological diversity among stem gnathostomes.

## Scientific guardrails

1. Do not say the animal literally flew.
2. Do not say the head’s function was directly observed; efficient cruising is inferred from CFD.
3. Do not portray the full living reconstruction as completely fossil-known. The headshield is fossil evidence; the body is reconstructed.
4. Do not claim the wing-like head evolved exclusively for hydrodynamics.
5. Do not call *Bataspis* a shark or a jawed fish.
6. Do not claim exact swimming speed, maneuverability, lifestyle, or habitat position.
7. Use “same broad principle as an aircraft wing” as an analogy, not identity.
8. The highest lift-to-drag result applies to the tested galeaspids/models, not every fish ever measured.
9. Treat the ground-plane experiment as evidence that the result was robust in the tested setup, not proof of where the living animal swam.
