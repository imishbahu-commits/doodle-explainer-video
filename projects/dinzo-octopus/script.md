# Why It Sucks to Be Born as a Giant Pacific Octopus

## Section 1 — The Beginning (0:00-1:00)

You are born in a dark underwater cave off the coast of the Pacific Northwest, one of tens of thousands of eggs hanging from the ceiling like a curtain of rice. Actually, tens of thousands is optimistic. Your mother laid somewhere between fifty and a hundred thousand of you. You are not special. You are a number.

You hatch at the size of a grain of rice. You have no mother waiting. Actually, you have exactly the opposite: your mother is still there, wrapped around the eggs, and she is starving to death on purpose. She has not eaten in six months. She will not eat again. By the time the last of your siblings breaks free, she will be dead.

You don't know any of this. You're a speck with eight arms and a will to live. You drift upward, away from the den, into open water.

Congratulations. You're alive.

For now.

## Section 2 — The Plankton Gauntlet (1:00-2:30)

For the first few months, you are not an octopus. You are plankton. You float near the surface, at the mercy of the current, a drifting fleck in the most crowded soup on Earth.

Everything eats you. Fish. Jellyfish. Other octopus larvae. Birds dive from above. Even things with no mouth want you dead. The ocean is a buffet, and you are the appetizer.

This is the slaughterhouse stage, and the math is not in your favor. Out of every hundred thousand of you that hatch, one or two make it. Read that again. Your survival odds are worse than a lottery ticket.

You do the only thing you can do. You drift. You eat what's smaller than you and avoid being eaten by what's bigger. You grow. Every day you get a little less helpless.

Weeks pass. Maybe months. Then, if you're still alive, you sink.

## Section 3 — Childhood (2:30-4:00)

You settle on the seafloor and start acting like an octopus. Your first job is the same as every octopus's first job: find a den before something finds you.

A den is just a hole. A crack in the rocks. An empty shell. A discarded bottle, if you're lucky. You squeeze into it and you learn the one trick that will keep you alive forever: you have no bones. The only hard part of your entire body is your beak. If your beak fits through a gap, the rest of you does too.

You are a liquid that can think.

You spend your first year hiding. You change color to match the rocks, because your skin is covered in thousands of tiny pigment cells called chromatophores, and you can turn them on and off faster than you can blink. You are the best camouflage artist on the planet, and you use it to be invisible.

You eat crabs, shrimp, and small fish. You catch them by pouncing, by reaching, by just being faster than they expect. You have three hearts pumping blue blood through your body. You have nine brains — one in your head and one in each arm. Two-thirds of your neurons live in your arms, which means your arms basically make their own decisions.

You are a genius. You are also a snack. A sea otter would eat you for breakfast. Literally.

## Section 4 — Becoming a Predator (4:00-5:30)

By year two, you are no longer the bottom of the food chain. You're a proper octopus now, maybe a foot or two across, and you've learned to hunt.

You hunt at night. You use your suckers — hundreds of them, each one able to taste and smell — to find a crab buried in the sand. You drift over it like a shadow. You spread your arms like a net. And then you pounce.

You can catch several things at once, because each arm can hold something different while you decide which one to eat first. You are not a predator with one weapon. You are eight predators sharing a body.

When a seal or a moray eel shows up, you don't fight. You run. Or you disappear into a hole no bigger than your beak. If a predator grabs an arm, you drop it. Seriously. You detach your own arm and swim away. It grows back.

You also have a final trick: ink. A cloud of black smoke that confuses the enemy while you jet away backwards. You have the best escape kit in the ocean, and you use all of it.

Life is good. You're smart, you're fast, and you're invisible. But you're also alone. You've been alone your whole life, and you will be alone for the rest of it.

## Section 5 — Territory and Growing Up (5:30-7:00)

By year three, you are huge. A full-grown giant Pacific octopus can stretch thirty feet across and weigh hundreds of pounds. You are the biggest octopus on Earth, and everything in your neighborhood knows it.

You keep a den. You decorate it with the leftovers of your kills — crab shells, clam shells, fish bones — piled outside the door like trophies. Other octopuses understand what that pile means. Stay out.

You're clever in ways that shouldn't be possible. You can unscrew a jar from the inside. You can solve mazes. You can carry coconut shells around and use them as armor. You can recognize the humans who feed you, and you hold grudges against the ones who don't.

But none of it matters, because something in your body is already counting down. You don't know it yet. But the clock started the day you were born.

## Section 6 — Love and Death (7:00-8:00)

Mating season arrives. You don't choose it. Your biology does. The optic gland — a tiny organ between your eyes — flips a switch, and your body starts planning its own funeral.

If you're a male, you find a female and you hand her a packet of sperm with one of your arms. And then you die. Within weeks. Your body just stops.

If you're a female, you survive a little longer, but only to do the hardest job in the ocean. You find a den. You lay your eggs — tens of thousands of them — and you hang them from the ceiling in long white strands. Then you stop eating.

For six months, you do nothing but guard them. You fan water over them with your arms so they get oxygen. You chase off anything that comes close. You watch them grow while your own body eats itself. Your arms shrink. Your skin goes pale. You waste away, one day at a time. Some mothers even start eating their own arms.

And then, one day, they hatch. Thousands of tiny grains of rice drift up out of the den and into the current.

You watch them go. You don't follow. You've done what an octopus does. You were born. You survived. You hunted. You hid. You mated. You made the next generation.

Your eyes close for the last time, and you sink to the floor of your den. A crab walks in a few days later. It doesn't know it's standing on a genius.

As far as endings go, it could be worse. At least you got to be the biggest, smartest, weirdest thing in the ocean.
