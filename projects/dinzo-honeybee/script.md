# Why It Sucks to Be Born as a Male Honeybee

## Section 1 — The Beginning (0:00-1:00)

You are born in a tiny wax cell, one of a few hundred boys your mother decided to make this spring. And here's the first weird thing about you: you have no father. Literally. Your mother never bothered to fertilize your egg. You came from an egg, all on its own.

What does that make you? It makes you a drone. Half a family tree. A mother, and a grandfather. Nothing else. The egg they built you from was unfertilized, which is the bee way of saying: this one's going to be a boy.

You take longer to grow than anyone else in the hive. Workers do it in twenty-one days. Queens in sixteen. You? Twenty-four. You are the slowest, laziest, biggest baby in the colony, and you eat like it.

You hatch into a world that already has an opinion about you.

Congratulations. You're alive.

For now.

## Section 2 — The Freeloader (1:00-2:30)

Here's your first problem. You can't feed yourself. You don't have the equipment. No pollen baskets, no stinger, no way to gather food. If a worker doesn't shove nectar into your mouth, you starve.

So that's your life. You sit in the hive, and the girls feed you. Mouth to mouth. You eat about three times what a worker eats, and you do nothing for it. No foraging. No building. No guarding. You can't even sting. You are, to be completely honest about it, a freeloader.

But the hive doesn't kick you out. Not yet. Because you have one job. One single, terrifying, stupid job. And the whole colony is willing to feed you until you get your chance to do it.

Your job is to mate with a queen. That's it. That's the entire reason you exist. You are a flying delivery system for sperm, and nothing else.

You spend your days warm, and fed, and useless. And somewhere outside, waiting for you, is the most crowded singles bar in the insect world.

## Section 3 — The Waiting (2:30-4:00)

When you're about a week old, you start flying. You leave the hive and you head for a place called a drone congregation area. No one is entirely sure how you find it. You just do.

It's high in the sky, above the treeline, and it's packed. Hundreds of drones. Sometimes thousands. Males from dozens of different colonies, all of you flying in circles, all of you waiting for the same thing.

A queen.

Queens are rare. A hive makes maybe a handful in a whole year, and each one mates only once in her life, on a single flight. She'll take about a dozen males. Maybe twenty.

So do the math. Thousands of you. One queen. A dozen winners.

The rest of you will die waiting. Most drones never mate. Never even get close. You will spend your whole life flying in circles, watching for a queen who will probably never come.

But you keep flying. Because what else is there? You are a lottery ticket with wings.

## Section 4 — The Moment (4:00-5:30)

And then, one afternoon, she appears. You feel her before you see her. Pheromones hit the air and every drone in the sky loses his mind at once.

The chase is on. She's fast, and she flies high, and she is not going to make this easy, because only the strongest, fastest drones are allowed to catch her. It's evolution's way of sorting the best genes from the rest.

If you're fast enough, you catch her. You latch on. And for two to five seconds, you have done the one thing you were born to do.

And then it kills you.

On the moment of finishing, a part of you — roughly the size of your own abdomen — is ripped out of your body. It tears away and stays inside her. There's a pop, so loud that beekeepers can hear it from the ground. You go limp. You fall out of the sky.

You're dead before you hit the grass. But hey. You won.

## Section 5 — The Price of Failure (5:30-7:00)

Most of you never get that moment. And if you don't mate, the colony has a different plan for you.

All summer, the workers tolerate you. They feed you. They clean up after you. They pretend you matter. But the whole time, the hive is doing math. Every mouth it feeds through winter is food it can't spare.

And then autumn comes.

One day, the feeding stops. No warning. No explanation. The workers just stop bringing you food. You sit there, getting hungrier, and you start to understand something the workers figured out weeks ago: you are now a liability.

Then they come for you. Workers grab you, they bite your wings, and they drag you toward the entrance. You struggle. It doesn't matter. They shove you out the door, into the cold.

And here's the cruel part. You can't feed yourself. You never learned. You were born dependent, and now you've been fired. You sit on the cold ground outside the hive, and you wait to die.

## Section 6 — The Cold (7:00-8:00)

You don't last long out there. Drones are terrible at cold. Tests have shown that a few hours in near-freezing temperatures kills three quarters of you, while workers don't even notice.

So you freeze. Or you starve. Or a bird eats you. Take your pick. Either way, your story ends the same way it began: alone, on the ground, a few feet from the only home you ever knew.

And the hive? The hive is fine. The workers settle in for winter, warm and fed, and they don't think about you again. You were never really one of them. You were an investment. And when the investment stopped paying, they cashed out.

In the spring, your mother will lay more of you. Hundreds more. And they'll live your exact same life. Eat, wait, fly, and die. The hive has been doing this for fifty million years, and it will do it for fifty million more.
