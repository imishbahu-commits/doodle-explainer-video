# Why It Sucks to Be Born as a Mosquito

> Dinzo-style second-person deadpan explainer. Landscape 16:9, crude
> hand-drawn doodles, flat colours, thick black outlines, hard cuts.
> One spoken beat = one image. ~8 minutes, ~1,750 words.
>
> Sources: Mosquito Magician (life cycle), Knox County Health Dept,
> Calcasieu Parish Mosquito Control, BetterTermite, Understanding Animal
> Research, CBC "The Nature of Things", Western Exterminator.

## Section 1 — The Egg (0:00-0:50)

You are born in a puddle. Not an ocean, not a nest, not anywhere special — a
puddle. A tire track. A clogged gutter. A bucket someone forgot outside last
July. Any inch of stagnant water will do. Your mother picked it because it's
wet and gross, which is the entire list of requirements.

She laid a few hundred eggs. You are one of them. A speck. A comma with a
point. You have no mother, no father, no name. You don't even have a face
yet.

You float on the surface with all your brothers and sisters, packed together
like a tiny raft of rice. Congratulations. You're born. For the next two days
you just sit there, doing absolutely nothing. This is the easiest part of
your entire life. Enjoy it.

## Section 2 — The Larva (0:50-2:20)

Then you hatch. And here's the first thing you learn about being a mosquito:
you breathe through your butt.

Not kidding. You are now a wriggler — a tiny grey worm with a snorkel sticking
out of your rear end. You hang upside down at the surface of the water,
sipping air through a tube on your tail. And the second anything bumps the
water, you dive. That reflex is basically your whole personality.

You can't bite. You can't fly. You can't even walk. You eat whatever floats
by — bacteria, algae, microscopic junk — by fanning it into your mouth with
little brushes. It's not a great diet, but it's free.

Over the next week or two you shed your skin four times. Each time you molt,
you're a little bigger. And each time, the fish are a little more interested.
Fish eat mosquito larvae like potato chips. So do tadpoles, and so do
dragonfly nymphs, and so does basically anything in the water with a mouth.

The dragonfly nymph is the worst. It hides in the mud and shoots its entire
face out of its head to grab you. You spend your whole childhood either
breathing through your butt or running from a face that shoots out of the
mud.

## Section 3 — The Pupa (2:20-3:00)

If you survive — and let's be honest, most of your siblings did not — you
turn into a pupa. This is your rest stage. By rest, I mean you now look like
a comma wearing a cape.

You don't eat. You don't even have a mouth for it anymore. You float at the
surface, and occasionally you tumble around just to show you're still alive.
Tumbling is the only move you have. You are a protein shake with a tail.

This lasts a few days. Inside that little casing, your whole body is being
taken apart and rebuilt. Everything you were — the wriggler, the
butt-snorkel, the brush-mouth — is being melted down and reassembled into a
flying machine.

Then the casing splits, and you crawl out of your own skin. Literally.

## Section 4 — The Adult (3:00-4:00)

Here's the part nobody mentions. When you first crawl out, you can't fly.
Your wings are soft and wet. You have to stand on the surface of the water —
on the water — balancing on surface tension, while your wings dry and
harden. If a fish shows up right now, that's it. Game over. You can't even
run.

But let's say you make it. Your wings harden. And then you get your job
assignment, and it was decided way back in the egg, like a lottery you never
entered.

If you're a male, your life is about to be short and strangely peaceful.
You'll drink nectar, you'll never bite a single thing, and in about a week
you'll be dead.

If you're a female, congratulations. You've just been hired to hunt the most
dangerous animal on the planet. Us.

## Section 5 — Mating (4:00-4:50)

Before the hunt, there's the swarm. At dusk, all the males in the
neighbourhood gather in a loose cloud and wait. Their entire short lives have
been building to this.

You — the female — fly through the swarm, and the males hear you. Not see
you. Hear you. Your wings beat hundreds of times every second, and every male
in that cloud is tuned to your exact frequency. To them, it's a love song. To
everyone else, it's the single most annoying sound on the planet.

One of them catches you, right in the air. And here's the thing about male
mosquitoes: after that, they're basically finished. Their one job was to find
you and mate, and they've done it. They go back to sipping nectar for a few
days and then die, probably thinking they nailed it.

You, meanwhile, are now carrying everything you need for the rest of your
life. You will never need another male again. Lucky you.

## Section 6 — The Hunt (4:50-6:30)

Now you're hungry. And this is where being a mosquito stops being a comedy
and starts being a horror movie — for you.

You need blood to make eggs. It's not optional. You can smell carbon dioxide
from over a hundred feet away. You can feel body heat. You follow breath,
sweat, and movement toward a giant warm thing that has no idea you exist.

Then you land. And this is the moment every mosquito dies a thousand times.
Because the thing you landed on hates you. Not dislikes you. Hates you. It
has spent its entire history inventing new ways to kill you. Slaps. Sprays.
Zappers. Screens. Candles. There are entire companies whose only job is to
murder you. There are scientists — actual scientists with degrees — who
spend their whole careers trying to make you extinct.

And here's the punchline. You are the deadliest animal on earth. You and your
kind kill around a million humans every year with the diseases you carry. The
most lethal creature in history... and it can be ended by a rolled-up
magazine.

## Section 7 — The Meal and the Eggs (6:30-7:20)

But tonight you get lucky. You land. You unsheathe the mouthparts — six tiny
needles — and you find a vein. You inject a little numbing spit, the stuff
that makes it itch later, and then you drink. You drink until you've taken on
about three times your own body weight in blood. You are now a flying red
balloon.

Somehow, you get away. You find a dark corner to digest, and over the next
couple of days your eggs develop. Then you fly back to the water — any
water — and you lay them. A hundred. Two hundred. A little raft of your own.

And then you do it all again. Find blood. Digest. Lay eggs. The whole cycle
takes about three or four days, and you'll do it as many times as you
survive. Most females get maybe three chances. The math on your life is
brutal: you were born to be swatted.

## Section 8 — The End (7:20-8:10)

Because in the end, everything is trying to kill you, and most of it
succeeds.

Bats eat hundreds of you in a single night. Birds pluck you out of the air.
Dragonflies — the same monsters that hunted you when you were a baby — are
still hunting you now. They eat you like popcorn. Spiders. Frogs. Fish, if
you fly too close to the water. Even the weather wants you dead. A hard rain
can end you. A dry week can vanish every puddle you need.

You are a creature whose whole existence depends on a quarter-inch of dirty
water and not being noticed. The female lives maybe a month. The male, about
a week. In that time you will have been a butt-breather, a mud-dodger, a
skin-crawler, a swarm-chaser, and a blood-thief.

You were born to be the deadliest animal on earth. And nine hundred and
ninety-nine times out of a thousand, you'll die before anyone even knows you
were there.

As far as being a mosquito goes... it sucks. And the worst part? The itch you
leave behind is the only thing anyone will ever remember you for.

Good luck out there. You're going to need it.
